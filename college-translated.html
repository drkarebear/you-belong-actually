Plain-language next steps

Open only the situation you need.

Each one gives you: what they probably mean, what to do next, and words you can borrow.

“Come to office hours.”Class
Translation: your instructor has time set aside specifically for students to ask questions, get clarification, or talk through a problem.
What to do next: check the syllabus or course site for the time and whether you need an appointment. Bring one question, assignment, comment, or confusing part.
You can say: “Could I use office hours to make sure I understand what you’re looking for on this assignment?”

More help with office hours and asking questions

“Check the syllabus.”Class
Translation: the answer may be in the course guide, especially for dates, grading, late work, attendance, communication, or class procedures.
What to do next: search the syllabus for the specific topic. If the policy is still unclear or your situation is unusual, ask.
You can say: “I checked the syllabus section on late work, but I’m still not sure how it applies to this assignment. Could you clarify?”
“Look at the rubric.”Class
Translation: the rubric shows the criteria your instructor is using to evaluate the assignment.
What to do next: compare your work to one rubric row at a time. Look for the first place where what you submitted and what the criterion asks for do not match.
You can say: “On the rubric, I’m not sure what the difference is between these two levels. Could you show me what would move my work up one level?”

If the rubric came with a grade that hit hard, start here

“Canvas says I got a zero.”Grades
Translation: the gradebook is showing zero points, but you still need to find out why. It could be a scored zero, missing work, a late-work rule, or a gradebook setting.
What to do next: open the assignment, read any submission status and feedback, check the course policy, and confirm what can still be done before assuming the answer is “nothing.”
You can say: “Canvas shows a zero for ___. I want to make sure I understand why. Is it missing, graded as zero, or something I can still address?”

Use the bad-grade recovery guide

“Prerequisite not met.”Registration
Translation: the registration system does not currently see the required prior course, placement, or other prerequisite on your record.
What to do next: check the course prerequisite and your record. If you completed equivalent work elsewhere or think the system is wrong, ask your college which office handles prerequisite clearance or transcript evaluation.
You can say: “Registration says ‘prerequisite not met’ for ___. I completed ___ at ___. Who can tell me whether that satisfies the prerequisite?”

Find the right campus office

“You’re on the waitlist.”Registration
Translation: the class is currently full and you are waiting for a possible opening. You are not enrolled unless your registration record says you are enrolled.
What to do next: watch your college email and registration status, and follow the college or instructor’s instructions for waitlisted students. Add-code and first-day procedures vary by college and class.
You can say: “I’m currently on the waitlist for your class. Is there anything you want waitlisted students to do before or on the first day?”
“You should see a counselor.”Planning
Translation: the question affects your education plan, major, transfer path, graduation requirements, course sequence, or another decision that depends on your individual record.
What to do next: make the appointment and bring the actual question. You do not need to arrive with your whole future figured out.
You can say: “I’m trying to decide ___. Can we look at how each option would affect my education plan?”

What counseling usually handles

“Check ASSIST.”Transfer
Translation: they want you to look at California’s official articulation information to see how community college courses may satisfy requirements at a transfer institution.
What to do next: use the correct community college, university, academic year, and major when you review an agreement. If the stakes are high, confirm your interpretation with counseling.
You can say: “I found the agreement in ASSIST, but I want to make sure I’m reading it correctly. Does ___ satisfy the requirement I need for ___?”

Decode ASSIST and articulation

“This class is transferable.”Transfer
Translation: a university may accept the course for transfer credit.
The hidden part: transferable does not automatically mean “counts for my major,” “meets my GE requirement,” or “works at every campus.”
What to do next: check the specific articulation or transfer requirement for your destination and major.

Decode “transferable”

“You’re on academic notice.”Standing
Translation: your cumulative grades or course-completion pattern has reached a level that requires attention under college rules. You may still hear the older word “probation.”
What to do next: read the notice carefully, find the specific condition it names, and meet with the campus office it directs you to. Ask what needs to change and by when.
You can say: “I received an academic notice and want to understand exactly what put me on notice and what I need to do next.”

Decode academic and progress notice

“You can still withdraw.”Enrollment
Translation: you may still be within a deadline to leave the class, but the result on your record and the consequences depend on timing and your situation.
What to do next: check the actual deadline and notation, your current course standing, and any possible effect on financial aid, enrollment status, or your education plan before deciding.
You can say: “Before I withdraw from ___, can you help me check what it would mean for my record, aid, and education plan?”

If you are deciding because you are behind, triage the class first

“There’s a SAP issue with your financial aid.”Money & aid
Translation: SAP means Satisfactory Academic Progress. Financial aid programs have academic-progress rules that can include GPA, how many attempted units you complete, and maximum timeframe.
What to do next: read the notice from your financial aid office and ask which SAP requirement is affecting you, what status you are in, and whether there is an appeal or other next step available under your college’s policy.
You can say: “My financial aid notice mentions SAP. Which requirement am I not meeting, and what options do I have from here?”

Notice the pattern?

College often gives students a short instruction that assumes they already know the system behind it. When that happens, translate the sentence into three questions:

  1. What does this actually mean?
  2. What action do I need to take?
  3. Who can confirm it for my situation?

That is not “needing too much help.” That is how you learn a system.
