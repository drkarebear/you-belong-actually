I’m Afraid to Ask for Help.

Asking can feel awkward when you are not sure what you are supposed to know already. Start with the problem. We can figure out the person and the words from there.

A more useful question:
“What information would help me move forward?”

Needing information, clarification, feedback, or support is part of navigating college. The useful move is getting the right kind of help from the right place.

Step 1

What kind of help do you need?

Choose the problem that is closest. You do not need to know the office name first.

I have a question about one class.

Start with: your instructor.

Examples: directions, feedback, missed work, grades, class policies, an assignment, or what to study next.

See ways to contact an instructor

I need help learning the material.

Try: tutoring, a writing center, math lab, library help, supplemental instruction, or your instructor.

You can use academic support before you are in trouble.

See what to say

I have a registration or education-plan question.

Try: counseling, admissions and records, or the office named in the notice you received.

Examples: prerequisites, adding or dropping, graduation planning, transfer requirements, or course selection.

See what to say

Life outside school is making school hard.

Try: your college’s basic needs, financial aid, disability services, student health, or other student support programs.

The right answer may be practical support, not a better study trick.

Step 2

If the question is about a class, the instructor is usually the best first stop.

You have more than one way to ask.

Email or Canvas message

Good when the question is specific or you need to explain something briefly in writing.

Helpful: include your course or section, name the assignment or issue, and ask one clear question.

Office hours

Office hours are time the instructor has set aside for students to ask questions, talk through work, or get clarification.

You do not have to be in serious trouble to use them.

Before or after class

Useful for a quick question. If the answer will take time, ask when the instructor would prefer to talk.

Appointment

If posted office hours do not fit your schedule, you can ask whether another meeting time is possible.

Borrow these words

You can keep the message short.

Change the blanks. Keep what sounds like you.

I do not understand part of an assignment.

Hi Professor [Name], I’m working on [assignment]. I understand [part you understand], but I’m stuck on [specific part]. Could you clarify what you are looking for there? Thank you.

I am behind and need to know what is still possible.

Hi Professor [Name], I’m behind in [course] and I want to make a realistic plan to catch up. Could you tell me which missing work can still be submitted and what I should prioritize first?

Need to sort the whole pile first? Use the falling-behind triage.

I do not understand feedback or a grade.

Hi Professor [Name], I reviewed the feedback on [assignment], and I want to understand how to improve. Could you help me understand [specific comment, rubric area, or question]?

I need to ask for a meeting.

Hi Professor [Name], I have a few questions about [topic]. I cannot make your posted office hours. Would there be another time when I could meet with you for about [10/15/20] minutes?

Tutoring is not a punishment

You can go to academic support with an unfinished question.

You do not need to arrive knowing exactly what is wrong. Try:

1
“I understand the first part, but I lose track when ___.”
2
“Can you look at what I tried and help me see where I went off track?”
3
“I have the assignment directions, but I am not sure how to start.”

When you do not know the office

You can describe the problem instead of naming the department.

Hi, I’m trying to figure out who can help me with [briefly describe the problem]. If this is not the right office, could you tell me where I should start?

You are allowed to start in the wrong place. College offices can be confusing even when you have been on campus for a while. A useful staff member can often point you toward the right next stop.

A 20-second check

Before you send the message

  • Name the class, assignment, or problem. Give the reader enough context to know what you mean.
  • Say what you already tried when it matters. That helps the person see where you are stuck.
  • Ask for one clear next thing. Clarification, a meeting, the correct office, or what to prioritize.
  • Send it. The message does not need to sound impressive. It needs to make the question understandable.

What if asking still feels uncomfortable?

You can feel uncomfortable and ask anyway. The goal is not to become fearless before you contact someone. The goal is to make the next move small enough to do.

Smallest possible move: copy one script, fill in the blanks, and save it as a draft. Then decide whether it is ready to send.

Asking is one way people learn how college works.

You do not have to arrive already knowing every rule, office, phrase, or process. Questions are one of the ways that knowledge gets built.
